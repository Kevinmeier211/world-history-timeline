## Kerma: production, provision and an object found at Abydos

The Met’s [Classic Kerma Beaker](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/545772), accession **20.2.45**, was reconsulted in its three Description paragraphs, Artwork Details and Public Domain/Open Access information. The parent later renewed the complete visible Description and Artwork Details after earlier direct access gaps. The existing **1265 × 1600** photograph was actually viewed; no fresh download-to-local byte comparison is claimed by the source researcher.

The catalogue’s **c. 1802–1640 BCE** range, pottery material, **10.8 cm** height and **14.2 cm** diameter belong to this vessel. The recorded findspot is **Abydos tomb 525**, excavated by Garstang in **1908**, rather than a documented Kerma workshop. Hand forming, red ochre, polishing and reducing conditions are the museum’s manufacture account; the opalescent band’s proposed coating remains tentative. The visible surface does not independently establish a firing atmosphere or recipe. A trader from Kerma living in Egypt is a proposed owner biography, not an identity proved by ceramic style. The existing visible catalogue range remains the basis for the inherited API end-date correction; this work does not claim a fresh API audit or consultation of the original grave report.

The Met’s [Sudan, 2000–1000 B.C. chronology](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/toah/ht/03/afs.html), published **October 2000**, was read again in its Upper/Lower Nubia labels, Overview, selected Key Events for **c. 2050–1500** and **c. 1550–1070 BCE**, and citation metadata. It supports a bounded account of trade, conflict, diplomacy, locally made and imported goods, and later Egyptian campaigns. It is an institutional synthesis, not a new excavation chronology. Its Classical Kerma convention, **c. 1750–1550 BCE**, is not silently harmonized with the older project table’s **Middle c. 2050–1750**, **Classic c. 1750–1480** and **Final c. 1480–1450 BCE** phases. Neither convention replaces the selected vessel’s date or the complete original case interval.

Three archaeological project accounts supply practical institutional and household context:

- Séverine Marchi’s [AIBL mission account](https://aibl.fr/fouilles/mission-archeologique-suisse-franco-soudanaise-de-kerma-doukki-gel-soudan/): selected _Présentation_ paragraphs 1–3, _La ville antique de Kerma_ paragraphs 1–3 including the workshop paragraph, and the opening _Doukki Gel_ discussion through the Classic Kerma nucleus. Author identification, bibliography and figure credits were also read in indexed text. The account explains how excavation changed the interpretation from an Egyptian trading establishment to the religious centre of an independent Nubian capital. Houses, royal and administrative buildings, audience space, chapels and craft installations support a concrete account of urban work. Their functions remain excavators’ interpretations; proximity to cult buildings does not by itself prove royal monopoly, quotas, distribution or individual worker status.
- The older mission [Sites account](https://kerma.ch/en/site/): selected City of Kerma foundation, changing defences, house/courtyard and domestic-annex paragraphs, with selected necropolis and Doukki Gel text encountered for comparison. Kitchens and silos make provisioning visible at a residential scale. A household census, full residential report or measured plan was not consulted. Later bread, beer and meat-offering workshops at Doukki Gel belong to Napatan/Meroitic contexts and are not projected backward into this case.
- The mission’s [History account](https://kerma.ch/en/history/): Location, chronology table, selected Kingdom of Kerma livelihood/ceramic-periodization paragraphs and selected Egyptian Occupation text. Herding, plant resources, hunting and fishing provide a broad livelihood account, not dietary percentages or a universal schedule. Pre-Kerma granaries are earlier evidence, not the contemporary city’s storage count. Strong environmental-collapse explanations and priority claims are not adopted.

These undated project pages are not assigned the consultation year as their publication date. The older site’s “current” research description concerns **2008–2011**. Newer AIBL text reports earlier Kerma-period levels at Doukki Gel; the older simplified foundation narrative cannot stand for the site’s entire history. No complete excavation volume, original faunal/botanical dataset, ceramic chemical analysis or full household distribution study was read.

The AIBL plan and other project figures have creator/mission credits, but no reusable licence was established and their actual plan pixels were not inspected. No Kerma source figure, measured geometry or image is copied or traced. The new making guide is an original explanation, described below.

Several leads remain outside the teaching account. The **2022 copper-alloy study**, DOI **10.1016/j.aia.2022.01.001**, returned 403 at the DOI/publisher; selected indexed text was available, but no PDF, tables or reanalysis. Its **48 objects** span long periods and cannot support a universal statement about Classical Kerma. The crucible paper’s PDF returned 502 and was not read. Marchi’s cereal-storage article was identified through an indexed first page and bibliographies, but the original PDF returned 404, an alternate host was inaccessible and a bounded download failed DNS; no body, plans or storage estimates were read. Its first-page **2017** and bibliography **2018** dates remain unresolved. None becomes a full-text authority through a search snippet.

## Nimrud: making a palace, royal claims and later orders

Michael Seymour’s [Nimrud](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/nimrud) was reread in its body and citation block. The source gives **November 2016**, correcting inherited April 2016 metadata. Selected use concerns the Northwest Palace, mud brick and local gypsum, carving/painting, protective and royal imagery, capital history and later occupation. The further-reading books and current site damage were not independently surveyed.

Seymour’s [The Assyrian Sculpture Court](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-assyrian-sculpture-court) was reread in its body and citation metadata: **December 2016, updated January 2022**, rather than the inherited October 2022 date. Quarrying, moving, carving and painting support an account of skilled work. The modern Gallery 401 combines material from different rooms; its tiles, ceiling and arrangement do not preserve an untouched ancient interior. The source itself calls the almost-70,000 banquet figure probably exaggerated, so it is not treated as a census.

The Met’s [panel with an eagle-headed winged figure](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/322614), accession **32.143.7**, has a catalogue date of **c. 883–859 BCE**, gypsum alabaster material and dimensions **236.2 × 177.8 × 10.8 cm**. The existing **1140 × 1600** photograph was actually viewed. Earlier direct renewal encountered a 429/tool failure; the parent subsequently read the official Description, Artwork Details and Public Domain/Open Access information successfully on 29 October. That success renews the named scope without erasing the earlier access history.

The bird/eagle head, wings, patterned clothing, bucket and cuneiform are visible. The figure faces left. The cone’s precise nature and protective/purification functions remain attributed interpretations. No exact original doorway is established here, and the recurring Standard Inscription context is not a newly transcribed individual panel.

Eleanor Robson’s [The Northwest Palace](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/nimrud/ancientkalhu/thecity/northwestpalace/index.html), last modified **18 December 2019**, was saved and read in _Managing the palace_, _Encapsulating empire_, _Receiving guests_, _Behind the scenes_, _After Assurnasirpal_, Image 1 and footnote 1. It connects rooms and courtyards with storage, administration, reception, cooking and continuing alterations. Room ZZ’s oven/cooking vessels and the later Room ZT4 archive help explain practical work. Room functions and staff identifications are interpretations rather than a complete staff roster. Its universal slave-labor wording is not adopted; the underlying Oates/Oates, Mallowan, Postgate and other volumes were not read.

### The palace plan is a modern composite source

The actual [BSAI/BISI Northwest Palace plan](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/nimrud/images/ancientcity/nwpalace-plan.jpg), Image 1 on Robson’s page, was downloaded and visually inspected. Its **460 × 926** source pixels contain a near-upward north arrow, slightly toward the left, and a **0–50 m** scale. Rooms/courtyards and Room ZZ are labeled. It also includes **Sargon prism**, **Adad-nerari III Palace**, tombs and later annexes: this is a composite excavated plan of a long-used building, not a single **883–859 BCE** phase or proof that every drawn space functioned simultaneously.

The [Nimrud Project copyright and licensing page](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/nimrud/aboutthiswebsite/index.html), also last modified **18 December 2019**, was saved and read. It explicitly releases images credited **BSAI/BISI** under [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/) courtesy BISI, while third-party images may have different terms. The copied plan remains **unmodified**. Attribution retains BSAI/BISI, Robson/the Nimrud Project and AHRC funding; rights do not transfer to unrelated illustrations.

The caption’s reference to **Oates and Oates 2001, figures 15 and 33**, is a book reference, not a demonstrated creation date for the modern plan. The exact plan creation year remains unknown. The linked 128 MB book and the Met’s 1980 _Assyrian Reliefs and Ivories_ volume were not downloaded or read. Small room labels need natural-size limits, zoom and a readable caption/text equivalent; enlargement does not recover detail or establish that the source meets the font floor for newly authored diagrams.

## Taharqo: temple institutions, specialised manufacture and funerary labor

The Met’s [Shabti of Taharqo](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/544883), accession **18.2.10**, was reconsulted in Description, Artwork Details and Public Domain/Open Access information. The existing **1048 × 1600** front photograph was actually viewed by the source researcher and parent. The visible catalogue gives **690–664 BCE**, granite described as a Nubian variety, and **20.3 cm** height. Its Nuri association is **probable**, not a documented precise excavation position. The visible date range remains distinct from the API’s repeated end-year error. No maker, exact manufacture year, later manufacture or reuse is established by the consulted scope.

The separate [Seti I shabti](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/544763), accession **26.7.919**, **c. 1299–1290 BCE**, was reread in Description and Artwork Details for general substitution, manual tasks and tools. It explains labor imagined after death, including cultivation, irrigation and moving sand. It is not a translation of Taharqo’s particular inscription. No audio was listened to; the page reported the transcript unavailable. Elite funerary equipment does not establish every household’s belief.

The [Ashmolean three-page information sheet](https://www.ashmolean.org/files/learnpdftaharqasshrinepdf) was read in text across **pp. 1–3**, with actual **p. 3** rendered and viewed. It supplies the **c. 680 BCE** Kawa temple/shrine orientation, Memphis craftspeople, offering imagery and priests’ repeated care for the divine statue. The visit/building narrative is the museum’s account, not a builder-authored diary. The sheet is undated: file creation in 2009 and modification in 2014 are not demonstrated publication dates. Its erroneous “2500 thousand miles” transport wording is not used.

The [Ashmolean gallery webpage](https://ashmolean.org/ancient-egypt-and-nubia-gallery) was also read in its historical body and relevant highlights. Its body puts the shrine around **680 BCE**, while the Ram of Amun highlight says **680 CE** and the shrine highlight separately gives **715–657 BCE**. The CE error is not adopted, and the broader catalogue highlight range is not substituted for the retained case. No Ashmolean photograph, map or relief illustration is copied; no item-specific reusable image licence was established.

Aleksandra Hallmann’s **2024** chapter, [The “Libyan Family” at Kawa: Fashion as a Political Statement of Taharqo](https://isac.uchicago.edu/publications/master-secrets-chamber-darkness-egyptological-studies-honor-robert-k-ritner-presented), in Scalf and Muhs’s _A Master of Secrets in the Chamber of Darkness_, Studies in Ancient Cultures 3, was read in selected printed **pp. 121 and 131–133** / PDF **152 and 162–164**. Printed **133** discusses **Kawa III line 22**, listing male and female servants among temple gifts. Those are mediated royal claims over people and provision. The underlying Macadam/Ritner publications were not freshly consulted; no translation of the Met figure, workforce count, universal worker status or payment system follows. The publisher states **CC BY-NC-ND 4.0**, the PDF front matter states © 2024 University of Chicago and figures have Griffith Institute courtesy credits; no chapter illustration is copied, traced or adapted.

### Sanam: production evidence with its own chronology

Rennan Lemos, Kathryn Howley, Siobhan Shinn and Shadia Abdu Rabo’s [Crafting power in Kush](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/crafting-power-in-kush-faience-production-and-state-control-in-the-napatan-region/A15CF88886CC39E3C6D4110DD58B94A5), DOI **10.15184/aqy.2026.10434**, was published online **19 October 2026**, _Antiquity_, First View, pp. 1–20. The publication date is separate from acceptance, deposit, server-version and consultation dates.

The publisher HTML Abstract, Introduction, New excavations, workshop section, production/consumption, state discussion, Conclusion and figure captions were read. Actual PDF text at **pp. 10 and 13** was checked for the ceramic dating and mould match; research extracts of other selected pages are not a whole-PDF reading claim. Underlying experiments, chemical analyses, Griffith/Reisner publications, Howley’s dissertation and supplementary datasets were not independently read or reanalyzed.

Excavations in **2018–2020** found three firing pits, two mudbrick basins and production debris. A clay mould from within the temple matches an **uninscribed green faience shabti fragment** from the workshop. This supports manufacture; temple organisation and royal funerary consumption are the excavators’ interpretation. It neither identifies the selected granite object with the mould nor locates its workshop.

The abstract calls the workshop **mid-seventh-century BCE**. PDF **p. 10** discusses later **eighth–seventh-century** marl-jar parallels and **seventh–sixth-century** local ceramic counterparts, then an Early–Middle Napatan use frame. These broad conventions are retained, rather than converted into one firing year, a named ruler’s complete production episode or proof that manufacture occurred wholly before or after 664 BCE. The article’s Dynasty 25 convention **c. 747–656 BCE** supplies the separate reference frame; it does not change Taharqo’s **690–664 BCE** case or encompass all the broader workshop comparisons.

Shell/recipe interpretation remains qualified: morphology-based identifications are not fresh chemical confirmation, every shell’s demonstrated provenance or a reconstructed Red Sea procurement route. The article itself discusses uncertainty over wider household/state organisation. A temple workshop is useful institutional evidence, not a complete account of Nubian social life.
