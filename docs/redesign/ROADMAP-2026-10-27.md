## What “finished” should mean

A reader should be able to locate a society or process in time and space, understand what was happening elsewhere, follow a meaningful historical explanation, inspect the evidence behind it, and return later with a stronger understanding. A family should be able to begin a useful short activity without understanding the entire interface.

The release should demonstrate these outcomes:

1. **Orientation:** a genuine world overview connects regions, historical sequences and selected changes through time. A year lookup yields useful, accurately dated context even when no case-study object matches that exact year.
2. **Depth:** important reference entries explain actors, institutions, mechanisms, change and ordinary life. Small object inquiries can stay concise, but must connect to that deeper context.
3. **Evidence:** consequential assertions have appropriate sources and locators. Interpretations, disputed dates, reconstructions and source-access limits are visible where they matter.
4. **Visual explanation:** maps, objects, diagrams, plans and documents answer historical questions. They are legible, traceable and usable on phone, desktop and print.
5. **Usable exploration:** overview → chapter → subject → world context → comparison → source → return works without accidental filtering, dead comparisons or loss of place.
6. **Learning:** short and extended investigations offer observation, explanation and later reflection. Saved notes can be revised and recovered.
7. **Inclusive scope:** the coverage ledger distinguishes a researched example from an adequate regional framework and records important missing histories explicitly.
8. **Reliable delivery:** the current application has meaningful automated checks and documented human verification limits. Packaging and publication status are precise.

Word counts, entity counts, screenshot counts, PDF page counts and test totals are supporting diagnostics. None substitutes for these outcomes.

### B2. Adopt layered explanatory writing

The minimum is not a fixed word count. The reader should understand the subject’s place in the world and be able to make a supported explanation afterward.

- **Invitation:** a short, concrete question and an accessible opening grounded in a visible object, place or human problem.
- **Orientation:** what/where/when, why this inquiry matters, and where it fits in a larger sequence.
- **Main account:** meaningful actors, institutions, daily activities, mechanisms, chronology and change. Include concrete examples rather than a sequence of generic caveats.
- **Evidence in use:** explain a source’s authorship, context and purpose, what it supports, and a specific limitation. Offer a readable excerpt/transcription or annotated visual where rights permit.
- **Interpretation:** distinguish stronger conclusions from plausible alternatives; explain real disputes instead of generic “we cannot know everything” language.
- **Connection:** at least one useful supported comparison or historical connection, plus the relevant reference entry or chronology.
- **Reflection:** a question answerable from what the reader actually saw and learned.

As a design guide, a major reference entry might use 400–800 words of core explanation plus an optional 800–1,500-word deeper treatment. A bounded object inquiry may need much less. These are capacity targets, not minimum quotas or permission to pad. Some topics merit multiple linked entries instead of one overlong essay.

Keep source criticism precise and nearby, but move repeated technical provenance/access detail into the evidence layer. For major syntheses, use appropriate current scholarship alongside primary, institutional and community material. Do not force every narrow object entry to have an arbitrary number of sources.

Review metadata should record actual source consultation, unresolved issues and the nature of the reviewer. Avoid automatic approval defaults. “Included in local candidate” and “historically reviewed” should not be one ambiguous state.
