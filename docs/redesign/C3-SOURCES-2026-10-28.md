Attaching diagrams to Qin and Ashoka reduces the media-less count while leaving their historical-object/inscription photograph gaps open.

The three place studies are original approximate locator maps:

- **Qin:** Xianyang capital area, Lishan mausoleum and Liye/Qianling archive. The Yuelu collection is not assigned an excavated location. No imperial boundary, official road or transmission line is drawn.
- **Ashoka:** Pataliputra and selected inscription settings at Kandahar, Girnar and Dhauli. The retained main atlas point at Sarnath remains one pillar-site context. Point distribution does not establish territorial control, one messenger’s itinerary or universal comprehension.
- **Athens:** Agora, Southeast Fountain House and Pnyx. The ASCSA end-of-fifth-century map was privately inspected for orientation, not traced or reproduced. The original locators are approximate, not a surveyed plan, household route or identification of the Met painting’s fountain.

Coordinates orient the reader to named sites/areas. They are not new archaeological measurements. The map controls preserve each named place when markers cluster on phones. Print retains named explanations and source references.

## Dates, comparisons and remaining research

All 77 existing dossier intervals and date explanations remain. Qin retains 221–206 BCE and its explicit 221–207 alternative; Liye’s mainly 222–208 documents and the mausoleum project beginning around 246 have separate clocks. Ashoka retains c268–232; the Mauryan reference uses rounded c320–185 orientation and exposes uncertain succession traditions. Its approximate Kalinga viewing year derives from the retained reign and eighth-regnal-year statement. Athens retains the sixth–fourth-century window; the c510–500 jar, 508 conventional transition, 451/0 rule, 403 reestablishment and c330 account are distinguished.

The new journey follows thematic order across different dates and places. It supplies no historical contact, causal route, uniform political development or ladder toward modern democracy. Comparisons identify practical tasks, authority and source limits. The three new glossary terms—standardization, dhamma and deme—support those reading questions without claiming a complete glossary redesign.

More original legal/epigraphic editions, regional reception, household experience, recruitment and coerced work, civic/religious roles, resident foreigners and enslaved people remain targeted research. These accounts need independent review for explanatory quality and source use. Adult/family sessions, screen readers, physical devices and physical print remain separate acceptance tasks.
